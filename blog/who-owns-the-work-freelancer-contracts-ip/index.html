Who owns the work when you hire a freelancer or agency?

You paid for the logo, the website copy or the software, so you own it, right? Not necessarily. In the UK and US, commissioning something does not automatically make you its owner. Here is how copyright ownership works for outsourced work and what to put in the contract, as of November 2026. General information, not legal advice.

The default rule in the UK

UK government guidance says that when you ask or commission another person or organisation to create a copyright work for you, the first legal owner of the copyright is generally the person or organisation that created it, unless you agree otherwise in writing. The UK Intellectual Property Office makes the same point for freelancers: copyright in what a freelancer writes belongs to them unless the contract transfers it. If the contract is silent, courts may find an implied licence to use the work for the purpose it was commissioned, but that is a limited right, not ownership.

This matters for logos, websites, copy, photos, videos and code. If you plan to adapt, resell or exclusively control the work, you want ownership or a clear exclusive licence.

Assignment must be in writing

GOV.UK guidance states that a transfer (assignment) of copyright is only effective if it is in writing and signed by or on behalf of the person transferring the rights. So an email saying "it's all yours" may not be enough, and a verbal promise definitely is not. A clear clause might say that the freelancer assigns all intellectual property rights in the deliverables to you on payment in full, and that they will sign any further documents needed.

Many people choose assignment on full payment so that the freelancer is protected until they have been paid. Ask for it in the quote stage; our project brief builder helps you describe deliverables clearly.

The US: employees, work made for hire and assignments

US rules differ. The US Copyright Office explains in its Circular 30 that a work can be a "work made for hire" in two ways: it is created by an employee within the scope of their job, or it is a specially commissioned work that falls into one of nine statutory categories and is covered by a signed written agreement saying it is a work made for hire. The categories include contributions to collective works, translations, supplementary works, compilations and instructional texts. A work that misses any of those tests is not a work made for hire, so for a typical logo or website from an independent contractor you generally need a signed written assignment instead. Where the work-for-hire route applies, the commissioning party is treated as the author.

Clauses to ask for

  • Deliverables defined. What exactly you are buying: files, source code, drafts, fonts.
  • Assignment or licence. Assignment of rights on full payment, or an exclusive, perpetual licence if you prefer.
  • Third-party material. Stock images, fonts, plugins and open-source code have their own licences; require a list.
  • Moral rights waiver. Where relevant, ask the creator to waive or agree not to assert moral rights.
  • Portfolio use. Say whether they may show the work.
  • Warranty of originality. That the work doesn't infringe others' rights.

If you have already paid without a contract

Don't panic, but fix it. Ask the creator to sign a short assignment covering the work already delivered, and record what you paid. Until then, you probably have at least an implied licence to use the work for the purpose it was made, but you may not be able to stop others using it or to modify it freely. Frequently asked questions

If I pay a freelancer, do I own the copyright in the UK?
Not automatically. Government guidance says the creator is generally the first owner of commissioned work unless the parties agree otherwise in writing, and an assignment must be written and signed.
What is a work made for hire in the US?
Either work by an employee within their job, or a specially commissioned work in one of nine statutory categories with a signed written agreement saying it is a work made for hire, per the US Copyright Office.
Is a licence enough?
It can be, if it is exclusive, covers the uses you need, and lasts long enough. Ownership gives more control, but a well-drafted licence may be cheaper to agree.
Do these rules apply in the EU?
EU member states have their own national copyright laws, which can differ on transfers and moral rights, so check local rules and use a clear written agreement.
